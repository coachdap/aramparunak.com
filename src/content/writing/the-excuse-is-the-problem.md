I don't take vacations from this. I don't cancel sessions because something came up. I don't sleep in, I don't check out, I don't phone it in. Not because I'm some machine running on pure self-discipline for its own sake, but because I have dreams and goals of my own — and I understand what's actually at stake here. Excuses won't get me where I'm trying to go. Only consistency will. I've seen too many kids get cheated out of something real because the adults in their lives couldn't hold a standard for more than two weeks, and I refuse to be one of those adults.

That's the thing nobody wants to say out loud. The bottleneck isn't the player. It's almost never the player. It's the environment the player goes home to.

I grew up without this. No trainer, no structured development, no one who looked at my game and said here's what you actually need to work on, here's the standard, here's why it matters. I figured things out the hard way and late, and I made it my life's work to build the thing I never had access to. Building it the right way — at the level I'm trying to build it — requires showing up at the same standard every single day. That matters to me personally, not just professionally. So when a parent comes to me with a list of reasons why their kid hasn't been in — the schedule was tough, we had a lot going on, it's been a busy stretch — I'm not going to pretend that sits fine with me. It doesn't.

The problem isn't the excuse. Excuses happen. Life happens. The problem is when the excuse becomes the posture. When the default response to friction is to explain why the thing didn't happen instead of asking what needs to change so the thing can happen.

There's a version of this conversation I've had more times than I can count. A parent reaches out, they want their kid to get better, they invest in training. And then the attendance gets spotty. And when I follow up, the reasons come. Every single one of them sounds reasonable in isolation. But when you add them up over weeks and months and years, what they actually spell out is: this is not a priority.

And here's what kills me — the kid knows. Players aren't stupid. They are watching everything. They see what their parents actually sacrifice for versus what their parents say matters. They see whether the commitment lasts past the first month when the novelty wears off. They see whether the adults around them treat an obstacle as a reason to stop or a problem to solve.

You want to teach your kid grit? Model it. You want them to keep showing up when it's hard? Show them what that looks like. You want them to take their development seriously? Let them watch you take their development seriously.

Consistency isn't a basketball skill. It's a character trait. And it gets taught — mostly by exposure, mostly by environment, mostly by watching the people closest to you handle the moments when it would be easier to bail.

I'm not saying every family can do everything. Resources are real. Schedules are real. Life is genuinely complicated. I get that. But let's be honest about what we're actually talking about most of the time. We're not talking about genuine hardship. We're talking about competing priorities — and the excuse is just the packaging we put around the fact that something else won. That's a choice. Own the choice.

I have places I'm trying to get to. Things I'm trying to build. Standards I'm trying to hold in my own life and work. I know from personal experience that the only thing standing between where I am and where I'm going is whether I show up consistently — not when it's convenient, not when everything else is handled, but especially when neither of those things is true. That's the standard I hold myself to. Most won't meet that standard.  That's their choice.  And so are the consequences that come along with it.

What I want — what I've always wanted — is for families to come at this the other way around. Not why we couldn't make it, but what do we need to do differently so we don't miss again. That's the whole game. That's what separates the players who actually get somewhere from the ones who had the talent and the access and still ended up with nothing to show for it. Not the reps. The orientation.

Because here's the truth: the work is available. It's ready when you are. The question is whether you're actually going to show up for it — consistently, not just when it's convenient — or whether you're going to spend the talkingabout all the things that got in the way.

I built this so kids could have what I didn't have. That means something to me. It connects directly to where I'm trying to go and what I'm trying to prove is possible.
