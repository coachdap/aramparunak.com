I've watched it happen more times than I can count. A kid gets to a new level, and they look like they've never played basketball before. From the outside it can look like a lot of different things.  Maybe they don't play hard.  Maybe they have a bad attitude.  Maybe they just can't seem to do anything right. From the inside, it's something more specific: they have no idea how to struggle.

They've never had to.

**The Damage Starts Earlier Than You Think**

Sometimes a kid was just the best player in every gym they ever walked into. Youth league. Middle school. AAU. Whatever rec league their parents signed them up for at age nine. And because they were the best player, adults around them made a choice — usually without even realizing they made it — to exempt them from accountability.

They were late to practice. Whatever. They gave half effort on the defensive end. Who cares, they're scoring 30. They argued with a teammate. Let it go, we need them Saturday. Every single exemption was justified by the same short-term logic: we need to win now, and this kid helps us win.

The problem is that every exemption is also a lesson. And the lesson is: the rules don't apply to you. Standards are for other people. When things get hard, you can opt out.

That's what actually gets installed. Not confidence. Not a winning mentality. A total inability to fight.

**What Accountability Actually Builds**

We talk about this constantly at Hoops College with our younger players. Go fast, fail often, have fun. We want kids to encounter failure while they're still in a safe environment, while the stakes are low enough that failure is instructive rather than crushing. The bench isn't punishment — it's information. You didn't meet the standard. Here's what it feels like. Now figure out what you're going to do about it.

That process — meet a standard, fall short, sit with the discomfort, try again — is the whole thing. That's not a side effect of player development. That's the mechanism. And when coaches skip it for their best player, they're not protecting that player. They're hollowing them out.

The true joy in competition is the struggle. I believe that. Not as a motivational poster slogan, but as something I've seen play out over and over. Players who've had to fight for their spot — who've sat the bench, who've had a coach tell them straight that they weren't performing — those players have something in them that the coddled star doesn't. They know how to respond when it matters. They've done it before.

**The Excuse Coaches Make**

I understand why coaches don't hold their best players accountable. Maybe someone above won't let them.  Maybe they don't want to lose.  Maybe they don't want them to transfer.  That doesn't mean I think it's ok. It means they have the wrong priorities in a very short-term view.   If you bench your best player and enforce the standard, you might lose the game. They might get mad. Their parents might get mad. You lose the game. But do you lose the whole season?  Or do you feed the killer that's inside.  And help them learn to compete for everything.  

Or maybe you don't lose at all.  Maybe the other players play harder because they know you have their back.  Maybe the best player responds because you show that you care about more than just about winning one game.  

Does your best player compete for every minute of playing time?  Every rebound. Every possession? In a world where transfers are easy and everyone has options, there's genuine pressure to keep the star happy at the expense of the standard. If they aren't happy, they might transfer.  Or they might appreciate that you care and you keep them for life.  

But let's be honest about what that pressure is really doing. It's asking you to choose short-term team results over long-term player development. And in doing so, it's also — and this is the part nobody says out loud — choosing short-term results over the long-term health of your program. Because if your best player knows the rules don't apply to them, every other player in that room knows it too. And they're drawing their own conclusions about what your standards actually mean.

You don't build culture by what you say. You build it by what you tolerate.

**What Happens When the Bubble Bursts**

The shock hits eventually. It always does. The kid gets to a level where they're not the best player anymore, and for the first time in their life, they have to fight. They have to earn minutes. They have to accept coaching. They have to sit down and do the uncomfortable work of being worse than someone else and dealing with it, physically and mentally.

And they don't know how. Nobody ever taught them. Worse — everything in their development actively taught them the opposite. That struggling means something is wrong. Is the answer to adversity blaming coach?  Is it blaming the event operator?  Is it blaming the trainer?  Maybe they are to blame. But if you have read anything, I've ever written you know that owning the struggle is the fastest way through it. 

I'm not interested in writing off those kids. Some of them figure it out. Some of them find a coach or a situation that finally holds them to something real, and they respond, and they become the player they always had the potential to be. That's a real thing that happens.

But it's so much harder than it needed to be. And it happens in spite of how they were developed, not because of it.

**What We Actually Owe Them**

If you care about a player — actually care, not "I need them to score 20 so I can win coach of the year" care — you hold them to the standard. You bench them when they don't meet it. You tell them the truth when the truth is uncomfortable. You risk the transfer conversation because you understand that a player who stays in your program because the rules don't apply to them is not a win.

The players who've been told the truth, held to the standard, and made to fight for their spot? They're the ones who come back and thank you later. The ones who were exempted? They're still out there, bouncing from program to program, wondering why it's never worked out.

They never learned to struggle. And that's on us.
