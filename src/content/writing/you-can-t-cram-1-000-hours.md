There is no shortcut that gets you to 1,000 hours in a weekend.

I want you to sit with that for a second before we go any further, because most of what's being sold to families right now — the showcase camps, the travel rosters, the "elite" exposure tournaments — is built on the fantasy that there is.

The Math Nobody Does

Let's say your child is in 7th grade. They're twelve years old, tryouts are around the corner, and you're suddenly serious about basketball. I understand the urgency. I'm not here to embarrass anyone. But I want you to do some math with me.

To have 1,000 hours of real skill work by now — not games, not standing in a layup line, not watching someone else shoot free throws — your child would have needed to start at six and average a little over three hours a week, every single week, for six years. That's 312 weeks without a gap. And that's the baseline. Not the prodigy track. Not the 10,000 hours you've heard about. One thousand is the floor.

And what I mean by skill work isn't vague. I mean passing and catching until it becomes automatic. I mean dribbling under pressure until the ball is an extension of the hand. I mean shooting — not from the three-point line to impress someone in the stands, but from five feet away, thousands of reps, until it's not even a thought. I mean layups from every angle, both hands, against defense. I mean footwork drilled until it stops being footwork and starts being habit.
That's what 1,000 hours actually looks like. Nobody sells it because it isn't exciting. It doesn't make for a great Instagram highlight.

What That Foundation Actually Buys You

Here's what those 1,000 hours translate to, and it is not what most people expect me to say.
It's not a scholarship guarantee. It's not a varsity roster spot. It's not a ranked recruiting profile.
It's confidence. Real confidence — the kind that doesn't need a perfect performance to survive. It's the understanding, developed over years and at a young age, that your body knows what to do. That when pressure comes — a double team, a critical free throw, a coach watching — your instincts don't betray you. Your foundation holds.

Players who have those hours in them don't panic the same way. They can be coached. They can adapt. They can fail in a game and come back the next possession, because they've failed and corrected thousands of times already in practice, alone or with someone who cared enough to give them honest feedback. They're ready. And they know they're ready. That knowing is worth more than almost anything I can give a player in a single session.

The Cramming Lie

Let me be honest about what I see constantly. Families want to "take it seriously" six months before tryouts — and the ask is to fix fundamental problems that have been building for six years. The player hasn't had real instruction. Nobody's counted their reps or corrected their footwork or made them uncomfortable. They've played games. Games are fine. Games are not development.

You cannot cram 1,000 hours into six weeks or six months. You cannot. The body doesn't work that way. Motor skills are built through repetition spread over time — practice, rest, sleep, and practice again — and they don't consolidate under panic. A player who has never had someone demand precision cannot absorb that precision in a month of rushed sessions and come out the other side transformed. It doesn't happen.

What does happen is that the player gets a little better, feels the gap between where they are and where they need to be, and either quits or keeps chasing shortcuts. Neither outcome serves them. And the shortcut usually looks like a showcase: paying to be seen before there's anything to see.

The Only Honest Answer

The honest answer is: start now, and make peace with the fact that you're building something that takes years.
If your child is four, that's where the fun is — seeing what a four-year-old can turn into with consistency is the joy of what we do. If your child is ten, you have time. If your child is twelve, you have less time, but you still have time — you just can't waste any more of it. If your child is fourteen and has never had real instruction, you're not starting late, you're starting. And that's still worth doing. But the conversation has to be honest about what the next two years can and cannot produce.

What good training produces, over years, is a player who is ready. Not perfect. Not guaranteed anything. Just ready. They know their game. They trust their body. They've been made uncomfortable in practice thousands of times and they've come out the other side.

That's the foundation. That's what 1,000 hours buys you.

The question isn't whether your child can put in 1,000 hours. Almost any kid with access and a half-decent environment can. The question is whether they start — and whether the adults around them are honest about what it actually takes.
