A player walked into the gym last night and said something that stopped me mid-sentence.

It wasn't profound the way quotes on a motivational poster are profound. It was honest. It came from someone who has been beating themselves up for years and, for one night, finally stopped.

"There's no way I'm worse than them."

That's the whole thought. Not "I'm the best" or "I've arrived." Just: I went to open gym, I looked around, and I know I'm not the worst one in here.

Let me tell you what that cost to say.

Four Years of Proving the Negative

This player has been coming to us for more than four years. Consistently. That matters. This isn't someone who showed up twice and disappeared. This is someone who has put in real time, real reps, real sessions, and we see it. The flashes are there: bright moments where you can see exactly who this player is becoming. This player does things most players can't do. They weren't like that on day one, and they've put up with a lot of me over the years.
The problem, and it has been a real one, is that none of it has ever been enough for the player. Every mistake confirms the story they've been telling themselves. Every bad stretch with a team or a coach becomes more evidence for the prosecution. We've heard the horror stories: bad situations, bad coaches, teams where the player felt invisible, misread, or just buried. Those experiences leave marks. You start keeping score against yourself, and you stop letting the wins count. When I say something positive, it sounds like I'm trying to make them feel better instead of being honest with them. I understand that. But I also know what I see.

Some of those situations I saw coming and couldn't head off. People are resilient. Thank goodness.

The flashes are there. The work is there. The future is bright. But the road is long, and this player has spent most of it staring at every pothole instead of looking at how far they've come.

What Clicked at Open Gym

So they went to an open gym. They'd been to plenty of these before: hyped-up sessions with roster spots supposedly on the line. Somewhere in this one, something shifted.

It wasn't a revelation. Just a quiet, honest look around and the recognition: I'm not worse than them. Today, right now, in this gym, I belong here.

Then something remarkable happened. They played. Really played. Not managed, not protected, not survived. Two hours of just going. And the result was the kind of positive that hadn't been showing up consistently.

That's what the mental weight had been costing. All that energy went into being not good enough, and when the weight lifted for one night, the player was right there.

The Standard That Actually Works

Here's what I keep coming back to. In development, we spend most of our time on getting better today. We keep the focus on today's work, not on the finished product or the vision of what's possible.

But a lot of players only see the ceiling. However far away it is, they aren't there yet, so they decide they're failing. Maybe they're comparing themselves to someone who already has offers, or to the player they think they should be by now. I'm not saying you shouldn't measure yourself against your goals. You should. You need a direction and an honest sense of what still needs work.

But for a player who has spent years convinced they aren't good enough, the ceiling can be crushing. When you're already losing the argument with yourself, being shown how far you have to go just confirms you'll never get there.
The standard this player found that night wasn't "be perfect" or "be done." It was simply: I'm not the floor today.
You're not going to step on me.

That's a competition you can win. And winning it, even once, teaches you something real: your own scorecard has been rigged against you.

What We're Actually Waiting For

I don't want to romanticize one good open gym. This player still has work to do. The flashes still need to become the baseline instead of the exception. The road is still long.

What changed is the player's relationship to the road. Once you realize you're not worse than them, once you let yourself look around and see that you belong, you stop fighting yourself for every step. Your energy goes into the work instead of the argument.

That's what four years of consistent effort was waiting for. Not for the skills to be ready. For the moment the player decided to stop losing to themselves.

Players aren't stupid. They know when they're good and when they aren't. Some act like they're better than they are. Some act like the opposite. The dangerous place is when they can't tell the difference anymore, when every piece of evidence lands in the same pile and the verdict is always the same. What broke that pattern wasn't a coach saying the right thing, a highlight moment, or some outside validation.

It was looking around, doing the math honestly, and deciding: not today. Today, I'm not the problem.

So go play. Find out what you've got. The ceiling will still be there. But for one day, tell yourself you're not worse than them, and go see what happens.
