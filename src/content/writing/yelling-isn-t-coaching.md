Every week I watch a coach point at a kid who just turned the ball over and say, "What are you doing?" Loudly. In front of everybody. And the kid stares at the floor, nods, and jogs back to the huddle knowing absolutely nothing he didn't know before the timeout.

That's not coaching. That's just noise.

**The Problem With Captain Obvious**

Here's the thing nobody wants to say out loud: identifying a mistake requires zero skill. None. You don't need to know basketball. You don't need a certification, a résumé, or a single day of experience playing or coaching the game. You just need eyes. If a kid misses a layup, everyone in the gym — the parents in the bleachers, the six-year-old eating Goldfish crackers in the corner — they all know he missed a layup. If a kid gets beat off the dribble, the opponent's grandmother knows he got beat off the dribble.

So when a coach's primary move is to holler the mistake back at the kid, they're treating that kid like he's stupid. And players aren't stupid. They know they messed up. They felt it. In most cases, they're already frustrated with themselves. What they don't know — what they genuinely cannot figure out on their own in that moment — is *why* it keeps happening and *what* they're supposed to do differently. That's the whole job. That's the thing the coach is supposed to provide.

But problem identification is easy. Problem solving is hard. And a lot of coaches — youth coaches, travel coaches, even some coaches at levels that should know better — have built their entire identity around doing the easy thing and calling it accountability.

**What Solving the Problem Actually Looks Like**

Real coaching isn't a reaction. It's a diagnosis followed by a prescription.

When a kid keeps turning the ball over, the question isn't "what are you doing?" The question is: What do I need to do to help you get better.  Is his handle breaking down under pressure because he hasn't trained that skill in game-like conditions? Is he catching in bad spots and putting himself at a disadvantage before he even starts moving? Is he trying to make the highlight play instead of the simple one. There are so many other options. Every one of those problems has a different solution and make take more time than one training session or one practice to solve. And none of those solutions come from yelling.

The same goes for defense. A kid getting beat off the dribble might have a footwork problem. He might have a positioning problem. He might have a habit of peeking at the ball. He might not know how to use the baseline as a second defender. These are distinct, correctable things. But you can only correct them if you've actually thought about *why* the problem is happening. If your move is to just say "stop getting beat!" louder, you haven't thought about it at all.

**The Kid Who Shuts Down**

I want to be honest about what the hollering approach actually produces, because I've seen it up close too many times.

When a kid gets berated for a mistake he already knows he made, one of two things happens. Either he gets tentative — he stops taking risks, stops making reads, plays scared — because the emotional cost of being wrong is now higher than the basketball value of being right. Or he goes numb. He learns to perform the head-nod, to survive the timeout, and to tune the coach out completely. Neither of those outcomes make him better. Both of them make the gym a worse place to spend time.

The best players I've been around are not the ones who had coaches who never got after them. They're the ones who had coaches who got after them with *information*. "You're watching the ball when your player cuts, see both." "Your pivot foot moved before you dribbled.  That's why you traveled." Specific. Actionable. Teachable. And then you have to work on it.  Not in the game. But here's the real problem.  These things don't just pop up in games.  They happen in practice too.  If you're not fixing it then, you shouldn't be surprised when it happens when there are real officials and a scoreboard.

That's the difference between demanding more from a kid and just venting your frustration in his direction.

**This Is What We Owe Them**

The truth is, coaching is a responsibility. When a kid shows up to your practice or your game, he's trusting that the adult in charge knows something he doesn't and can help him close that gap. If your feedback is functionally identical to what the kid could get from any random spectator — "you missed that!" "that was a bad pass!" — you're not holding up your end of that deal.

Tough coaching should be tough to receive because the standard is high and the feedback is honest — not because the volume is loud and the emotion is hot. Raising your voice at a mistake you can't actually explain how to fix isn't accountability. It's negligence dressed up in intensity.

The kids who get better are the ones with coaches who can answer the follow-up question: *okay, so what do I do about it?* If you can't answer that, stop coaching or get better. The kid standing in front of you deserves a coach who can help them, not you.
