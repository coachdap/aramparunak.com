I had a player walk — eleven years old, three years of organized basketball — and I asked him to do a left-handed layup. He looked at me like I'd asked him to recite poetry in Latin.

He didn't know how. He had never been asked to try.

That moment doesn't just bother me. It genuinely makes me angry. Not at the kid — the kid did exactly what kids do. He went to practice, he ran the drills, he played in the games, and nobody ever stopped him long enough to say: *you have to be able to finish with your other hand.* He did what was comfortable. He did what he was allowed to do. That's on us — every adult who stood in a gym with him for three years and let it slide.

**This Is What's Wrong with Youth Basketball**

People ask all the time what's wrong with youth basketball. The honest answer is: a lot. But the left-handed layup is the symptom I keep coming back to, because it's so basic and so avoidable and so obvious — and yet it keeps showing up.

We teach our five-year-olds to finish with their non-dominant hand. They can't do it well. That's fine. They're five. But they know they're supposed to. They know it exists. They work on it. So when an eleven or twelve-year-old shows up having never once been asked to attempt it — never, in three years of organized ball — what does that tell you about the three years?

It tells you the three years were mostly garbage. Not because nobody cared. I'm sure people cared. But caring isn't enough. You have to actually know what a player needs to learn, and then you have to teach it and hold them accountable to it, whether it's convenient or not.

**Whose Fault Is It?**

Here's where people get uncomfortable, so let me be direct.

It is not the player's fault. The player is a child. Children do what adults structure and allow. If no one ever said "go left," the player went right every single time, and that is completely predictable. You cannot hold a twelve-year-old responsible for curriculum decisions made by the adults around them.

So whose fault is it? It's distributed, which is part of why nothing changes.

It's on the coaches who ran practice for three years and filled the time with plays and scrimmages and never drilled fundamental skill development in any serious way. It's on the organizations that hired those coaches, gave them a gym and a schedule, and then asked zero questions about what actually happens in practice. And yes, over time, it becomes something parents need to pay attention to — not because parents should have to, but because the system isn't protecting their kids' development the way it should be.

The question I keep asking is: what exactly *have* you been doing for three years? If a player comes out the other end unable to execute one of the most basic skills in basketball, something went very wrong with how that time was spent. You were either teaching plays, running 5-on-5, or doing drills that had no transfer to actual skill. That's a failure of standards. And most youth basketball organizations have no standards worth defending.

**Age Is Not the Excuse**

I hear this: "They're just kids, it's fine, they'll learn eventually." 

No. Age is not the excuse here. A ten-year-old has the strength to lay the ball up with their left hand. Maybe they need to develop the coordination but they are fully capable of doing it. They don't do it because nobody made it a priority. The window for building foundational movement patterns is not infinite. The longer a player goes using only one hand, the more hardwired that habit becomes, and the more work it takes to undo it later. Neglect at eight makes your job much harder at fourteen.

What's actually happening in most youth programs is this: the games are fun, the parents are happy watching action, the coaches feel busy running drills that look like basketball — and nobody stops long enough to ask whether any actual skill is being installed. The scoreboard gets all the attention. The player's long-term toolkit gets none.

**What We Actually Do**

When a brand new player comes to us — someone who has never touched a basketball — I'm not surprised when they can't do much. They're new. That's the job.

But when a player walks in with three years of organized basketball on their resume and they've never been asked to finish with their left hand, that's a different problem. Now we're not just building a foundation. We're first convincing the player that the right hand isn't the only option, then we're rebuilding habits that have had three years to calcify.

It's fixable. We fix it. But it shouldn't need fixing.

The standard should be clear, it should be communicated, and it should be enforced from day one — not because kids need to be perfect, but because they deserve adults who know what they're doing. A player who spends three years in a gym and leaves without being able to finish with both hands was let down. Period.

That's worth being mad about. And I'm livid.
